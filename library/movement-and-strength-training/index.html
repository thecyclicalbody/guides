Movement and strength training

Movement supports your blood sugar, mood, sleep and stress resilience. Two kinds of movement do most of the work: walking every day, and strength training at least twice a week. Very intense training is a different story: when your energy is low or your stress load is high, it adds to the stress your body is already dealing with.

Why strength training matters for women

Estrogen helps you keep muscle and protects your bones, because it slows down the cells that break bone down. When estrogen is low for a longer time, for example when your period stays away because of under-eating, intense training or stress, you lose bone faster. Among athletes without a period, 22 to 50% have reduced bone density, compared with about 12% of women in general. Around perimenopause estrogen falls for everyone: in one Finnish study, women lost 0.5 to 1.5% of their muscle mass in just over a year during the transition to menopause, and the women who were more active lost less.

Strength training works on the same systems from the outside:

  • Muscle. Muscle only stays when you use it with enough load. More muscle supports your metabolism, strength and posture at every age.
  • Bone. Bone adapts to the forces it receives. Strength and impact training improve bone density in women before menopause, and in a trial of women after menopause with low bone density, heavy lifting twice a week increased bone density in the spine by almost 3%, while it dropped in the comparison group.
  • Blood sugar. Muscle is one of the main places where sugar from your blood is taken up and stored after a meal. More active muscle means steadier blood sugar.
  • Mood. In an analysis of 33 trials, strength training clearly reduced depressive symptoms, with the largest effect in people with mild to moderate symptoms.

How to start

  • Train at least twice a week and work all major muscle groups: legs, hips and glutes, back, chest, shoulders and core.
  • Start with exercises you can do well, such as squats, lunges, hip bridges, rows, push-ups (against a wall or bench is fine) and planks, and learn the technique before adding weight.
  • Make it gradually heavier. The last two or three repetitions of a set should feel challenging but doable with good form.
  • Add some impact for your bones if your body allows it, for example small jumps, hopping or skipping rope for a few minutes.
  • Eat a protein-rich meal within a few hours after training, so your muscles have the building blocks to recover.
  • Guidance from a trainer or physiotherapist is worth it if you are new to lifting, have pelvic floor complaints, are pregnant, have joint problems or have been told you have low bone density.

Other movement

  • Walk every day, about 60 minutes in total. That can be 10 to 15 minutes after each meal, a few shorter breaks or one long walk.
  • Break up sitting with 5 to 10 minutes of movement every hour.
  • Low-impact classes such as Pilates or yoga support strength, mobility and recovery.
  • Pause for now: HIIT, long intense runs and training on an empty stomach, until your energy is stable.
  • Follow your cycle: many women have more energy for heavier training in the first half of the cycle and need more recovery in the days before their period.
  • Keep it enjoyable: dancing, hiking, skiing, surfing or swimming all count.
