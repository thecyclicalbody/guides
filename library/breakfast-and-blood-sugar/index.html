Meals and blood sugar

Breakfast and blood sugar

In the first hour after waking, your body releases a peak of cortisol to get you going. A breakfast with enough protein and fat in that window gives your body the fuel it is asking for, so it does not need to keep cortisol high to keep your blood sugar up. A stable morning makes the rest of the day more stable: fewer energy dips, fewer cravings and a calmer stress response.

  • Eat within 60 minutes of waking, with at least 30 g protein (see Protein).
  • Three meals a day, no skipping. Long gaps without food are a stress signal for your body, especially when your energy is low. This also means no intermittent fasting; a natural overnight fast between dinner and breakfast is more than enough.
  • Bridge long mornings with a snack. If you start early, a snack with protein and fat halfway through the morning keeps your blood sugar steady until lunch.
  • Move after meals. A 10 to 15 minute walk after your meals helps your muscles take up sugar from your blood.

Eat more earlier in the day. Your body handles food differently depending on the time of day. In the morning and early afternoon your cells respond well to insulin, the hormone that moves sugar from your blood into your muscles and liver. Toward the evening that sensitivity drops, so the same meal raises your blood sugar higher and for longer at dinner than at breakfast. That is why it helps to make breakfast and lunch your biggest meals, with most of your carbohydrates, and to keep dinner lighter. A lighter dinner also leaves your body more room to rest and repair during the night.
